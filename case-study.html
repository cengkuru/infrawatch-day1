Case studies · proposed working definition

A case study explains one project's record; the scores only compare it

The assessment gives every project the same 40 questions so countries can be compared. A case study takes one assessed project deeper: why its disclosure record looks the way it does, who decides what gets published, and what could plausibly change it. It is built on the project's scored record, never instead of it.

Three things make it a case study

It starts from an assessed projectThe subject comes out of the scored sample, carrying its full record: scores, evidence states, flags and sources. A story about a project nobody assessed is journalism, not a case study of this assessment.
It explains, not just describesBeyond what was and was not published, it maps who benefits, who decides, and which institutional constraints shape the record: the actors and incentives behind the scores, and the plausible actions that could move them.
It is verified before it travelsClaims are checked against source documents, ideally loan-agreement-grade, before the case study is used publicly. Until then it is a teaching sample, and it says so.

The development process proposed, for CIPE confirmation

1
Select from the scored sample, and say why

Choose a project whose record raises a question worth answering: an instructive gap, a strong contrast, a pattern other countries will recognise. Record the reason for the choice and link the case study to the project in the assessment database. Never choose a project because its material is easiest to gather; that is the same availability bias the sample rules exclude.

2
Start from the assessment record

The scored 40 points, the evidence states, the flags and the source trail are the case study's skeleton. What was published, what was searched for and absent, and what was only findable from funders is already established evidence; do not re-litigate it, build on it.

3
Add the explanatory layer

Map the actors and institutions around the record: which authority owns publication, who benefits from opacity or disclosure, what fiscal, legal or political constraints operate, and what the project's financing form implies for which records should exist. This is where scores become an account of why.

4
Verify against source documents

Check the narrative's material claims at source: contracts, loan agreements, audit reports, official gazettes. Label every claim that has not reached that grade, keep both figures where sources conflict, and never present an unverified teaching sample as an audited finding.

5
End on plausible action

A case study earns its place by naming what could change: which record, published by which authority, would move which scores. The test of a finished case study is that a reader knows what to ask for next, and from whom.

The trap: the case study drifting free of the assessment. If its claims cannot be traced back to scored points, sources and evidence states, it stops being evidence and becomes opinion with a project name attached; and if it quietly contradicts the scored record, one of the two is wrong and the database is where that gets settled, not the prose.

Where this connects

Case-study linkage is one of the six retention fields in the assessment database. The findings ledger holds early, unverified material on its way to case-study grade. The full assessment process shows where case studies sit in the method: after scoring, never instead of it.
