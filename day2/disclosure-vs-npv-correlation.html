In 2023, Nigeria announced a $2 billion solar manufacturing project with Chinese partners. Three years on, the financing terms have never been published. So nobody, not a minister, not an auditor, not a citizen, can say whether it was worth it.

You cannot assess a project you are not allowed to see.

Publishing a project's financing does not make it good value-for-money. But it is the only thing that lets anyone assess whether it is. Here are ten flagship projects. Watch which ones can even be judged.

Of ten flagship projects, only six could be assessed up front. Three were judged only after they had run. And one cannot be judged at all.

exception one · published, yet a bad deal

Sankofa: you could see the trap. It went ahead anyway.

$250ma year

A take-or-pay clause locks Ghana into paying for gas it does not use, about this much, every year. Because the terms were public, the cost could be measured up front.

Transparency reveals the trap; it does not prevent it. Publishing the terms did not make Sankofa a good deal. It only made the bad deal visible, which is the most anyone can ask of disclosure.

exception two · never published, the wall

NASENI: no terms, so no verdict is possible at all.

Its financing was never published anywhere official. No one, expert or citizen, can say whether it is good value-for-money. Withholding terms is not a footnote. It is a shield against assessment. That is why NASENI is the one project no chart can place.

So, in one breath

Transparency does not make a project good. It is what lets anyone find out whether it is. Where the terms are public, a bad project like Sankofa can at least be seen. Where they are withheld, a project like NASENI cannot be judged at all. And the pattern that looks like "transparency brings value" is mostly the funder, not transparency itself.

How we scored this, and how it would be made rigorous

What this is. A teaching probe, not an empirical finding. Ten projects, scored by an analyst from public records (AidData, press, the Nairobi Day-2 case studies), not from a completed InfraWatch assessment or from loan-agreement-grade cash flows. It shows how disclosure and value relate, not how strongly. A correlation coefficient on ten hand-scored points would imply a precision the data does not have.

The hero numbers, from the table below. Of ten projects, value was computable from disclosed terms for six; for three the verdict came from outcomes leaking out (terms confidential); for one (NASENI) no assessment was possible at all.

How it would be made rigorous.

  1. Measured disclosure, not hand-coded. Pull real IDS scores from a live portal (the Ghana OC4IDS portal covers STMA, 28 projects with genuine disclosure data) instead of coding headline megaprojects by judgement.
  2. Hold the funder fixed. Compare projects with the same funder and varying disclosure, so the funder confound is held constant. This is exactly what the "show the funder" split makes visible.
  3. Value from the agreements. Real cash flows from the loan agreements, discount rate = government long-term bond at signing, social value capped 15%. Mark computed vs estimated so the gap stays visible.

Method, in full. InfraWatch, developed by CIPE (Center for International Private Enterprise), NPV toolkit led by IEA-Kenya. Three legs: a transparency assessment that maps to the CoST IDS, political-economy analysis (PEA), and net present value (NPV). This probe uses the disclosure and NPV legs. Disclosure = analyst coding of CoST IDS data-point coverage from reported records. Value = the 16-step NPV toolkit applied to reported terms where they exist, and an outcome-based verdict where they do not. Inputs from the Nairobi Day-2 case studies (AidData + press; treat as reported, confirm against loan agreements). The NASENI 85% figure is officially attributed but not officially published. n=10.
