Disclosure makes value testable.

Making a deal public does not make it a good deal. But it is the only way anyone can check whether it is. This page lines up ten infrastructure projects by how much of the deal was made public, against whether the deal looks worth the money. The pattern looks like "openness brings good deals." It is mostly something simpler: who lent the money. Seeing that clearly is the point.

Each dot is a project. Move right for more public information about the deal. Move up for a deal that looks more worthwhile. Colour shows who mainly paid for it. The projects that sit where you would not expect are the ones that teach you the most.

Two things the shapes tell you that the slope hides

Not every dot was judged the same way. For the circles (like Azura and Sankofa) we could actually work out the value, because the deal's terms were public. For the triangles (like SGR and Addis) we could not: the terms were kept secret, so all we have is what the project did in the end. The chart puts both on one line. The shapes are there so you do not mistake "we saw how it turned out" for "we checked the numbers."

The clearest sign that openness does not guarantee a good deal is Sankofa: a circle that sits low in the harmful zone even though its terms were public. Because the terms were public, the bad deal could be measured, and it still went ahead. Making it public let people see the trap. It did not stop them walking into it.

02The exceptions are the lesson

With only ten hand-scored projects, drawing a line through them would over-claim. The honest move is to look at the projects that land where you would not expect, because that is where the real lesson sits.

Public · looks worthwhile

Azura-Edo power plant

Won through open competition, with the World Bank and 16 named lenders. This is the "expected" outcome, but it mostly shows the effect of a careful lender, not proof that openness by itself created the value.

Public · still a bad deal

Sankofa gas

The case that breaks the rule. The terms were public, including a clause that locked Ghana into paying for gas it does not use (about $250m a year). Being able to see the trap did not stop the country walking into it.

Hidden · the failure showed later

Addis light rail · Kenya SGR

A railway running just 8 of its 41 trains, and another whose loan is worth roughly 5% of Kenya's whole economy and is straining the rail company's finances. The deals' terms stayed secret; we only know they went wrong because the results became visible afterwards.

Hidden · cannot be judged at all

NASENI solar

The honest blank. The financing terms were never published anywhere official, so there is no way to test whether the deal is good, and not even a track record yet. You cannot judge a deal you are not allowed to see, and that is the whole point.

04What this probe actually shows

Three things the ten projects support. None of them is "disclosure predicts value." The finding is narrower, and sharper:

Three claims

1. Disclosure decides whether a project can be judged, not whether it is good. The clearest pattern is not that hidden projects are bad. It is that a hidden project like NASENI cannot be assessed at all: you cannot weigh a deal's costs against its benefits when the financing terms are not public. Disclosure is the entry ticket for testing value.

2. The upward slope is mostly about who lent the money, not about openness. Lenders like the World Bank tend to publish more and offer better terms; Chinese and commercial lenders tend to do neither. Because those two things come together, the chart makes openness look more powerful than ten projects can prove. To pull openness apart from the lender you would need a much bigger set of projects, all with the same kind of lender.

3. Openness shows you the problem; it does not fix it. Sankofa is the proof: its terms were public, so the bad deal could be seen and measured, and it went ahead anyway. Being able to see the trap is not the same as avoiding it. What limits whether a deal can be judged is what gets published, not the maths.

Methodology and how this would be made rigorous

What this is. A teaching probe, not an empirical finding. Ten projects, scored by an analyst from public records (AidData, press, the Nairobi Day-2 case studies), not from a completed InfraWatch assessment or from loan-agreement-grade cash flows. It is meant to show how disclosure and value relate, not to measure the strength of that relationship. A correlation coefficient on ten hand-coded points would imply a precision the data does not have.

How it would be made rigorous. Three steps turn this from a teaching artifact into evidence:

  1. Use measured disclosure, not hand-coded. Pull real IDS scores from a live portal (the Ghana OC4IDS portal covers STMA, 28 projects with genuine disclosure data) instead of coding headline megaprojects by judgement. Smaller works, but actually measured.
  2. Hold the funder fixed. Compare projects with the same funder type and varying disclosure (e.g. all PRC). Only then does the remaining disclosure variation mean anything, because the funder confound is held constant.
  3. Compute value from the agreements. Real cash flows from the actual loan agreements, discount rate set to the government long-term bond rate at signing, social value capped at 15%. Mark every point as computed or estimated so the gap between the two stays visible.

Method, in full. InfraWatch, developed by CIPE (Center for International Private Enterprise), with the NPV toolkit led by IEA-Kenya. It runs three legs: a transparency assessment that maps to the CoST IDS data points, political-economy analysis (PEA), and net present value (NPV). This probe uses the disclosure and NPV legs only. Disclosure axis = analyst coding of CoST IDS data-point coverage from reported records. Value-signal axis = the InfraWatch / IEA-Kenya 16-step NPV toolkit (discount rate = government long-term bond at signing, social value capped 15%) applied to reported terms where they exist, and an outcome-based verdict where they do not. Project inputs from the Nairobi Day-2 case studies (AidData + press; treat as reported, confirm against loan agreements).
