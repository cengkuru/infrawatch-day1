Project eligibility · a one-page test

Two questions decide entry; the third decides the track

Every candidate project takes the same test before any scoring starts. The first question causes the most surprising exits, real foreign money but no public infrastructure, so it comes first.

The test

Follow one project through. The red exit is where most candidates leave: real foreign money, but no public infrastructure for the transparency questions to attach to.

In the mapping report this test is "two questions plus one financing record": entry is decided by questions 1 and 2 alone, question 3 never keeps a project out (it only decides which questions the project is scored against, and delivery model is classified separately from the foreign-funded designation, which is analytical, not legal), and every passing project builds the attribution record: each financing party's identity, domicile and ultimate control, with contractor origin recorded separately.

After eligibility, the workbench runs the next step interactively on a real project: what financing type it carries, which records that type should leave, and whether its value can be judged from them.

The common trap: a foreign builder is not a foreign financier. A construction contract, however large, is payment for work, not money at risk, so it never satisfies question 2, and the builder's flag never decides a project's classification either.

Each question in detail

What counts, what does not, and the discipline that keeps each answer defensible.

Question 1

Does it deliver a public infrastructure asset or service?

CountsRailways, roads, bridges, ports, airports, power plants and grids, water systems, public buildings. Who owns or finances it does not matter at this step.
Does not countFactories and manufacturing plants, commercial property, extraction of oil, gas or minerals. A commercial asset stays commercial even when a public body owns it; public ownership feeds question 3, never question 1. When a sector fits neither list, flag it rather than decide silently.
Why it mattersThe transparency questions follow a public project cycle. A private facility has no public procurement or delivery record to check, so its scores would not mean anything.
Question 2

Is the foreign financing real and documented?

CountsA named foreign lender, a named equity or ownership stake, a grant that funds the asset itself, or a guarantee: equity, debt, grants, guarantees or another financing instrument. Signed, and traceable to a source.
Does not countConstruction contracts, equipment supply, consultancy or design work, feasibility studies, and financing that is announced but not signed.
Due diligenceName the financier from a source; do not infer it. Signed means a citable source reports the agreement as signed; eligibility never requires the document itself, since how findable the documents are is what the assessment measures. Record the financing form for every project, because disclosure expectations differ by form. The full boundary rules live in what counts as foreign financing and the six financing forms.
Question 3

What role does the public sector play?

Owner or buyerA government body or state-linked company owns the asset or procured it. Score against the standard questions. Where state linkage is debatable, the deciding question is responsibility: which entity answers to the public if the project fails.
Long-term private partnerA private partner finances, builds or operates under a long-term contract and carries real risk. Concessions and power-purchase arrangements sit here; the usual name is a public-private partnership (PPP). Score against the standard and the PPP questions.
Facilitator onlyGovernment approves, regulates or promotes, but owns nothing and buys nothing. Flag the project; whether and how these are scored is being confirmed with CIPE. Until that ruling lands, keep the project listed and flagged, hold it out of scoring, and prefer a buffer substitute where one exists.

Choosing among projects that pass

Four proposed rules govern which passing projects make a country's list, which holds twenty projects. They exist so a defensible list cannot be mistaken for a convenient one.

  1. Seek a reasonable spread of financing countries where the available projects allow it.
  2. A sample dominated by one country is acceptable where it reflects what actually exists. Where spread and reality conflict, reality wins, and the documentation shows it.
  3. Record the pool you drew from, the criteria applied, and the reason each final project was chosen.
  4. Never pick a project because its information is easy to find. Availability of data is not a selection criterion; it is what the assessment measures.
Older projects window being confirmed
A project completed outside the preferred window may stay only as a flagged exception, with the reason no newer substitute exists. The exact window is being confirmed with CIPE and will be recorded here when settled.
Buffer list
Keep a short list of substitutes that also pass the test, so a project that fails later scrutiny can be replaced without reopening the whole selection. How the buffer works.
