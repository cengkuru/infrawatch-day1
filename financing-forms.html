Financing forms · a one-page guide

How a project is paid for predicts what will be hidden

The financing form is the first fact to record about any project. It tells you what the public owes back, which records should exist, and where the paper trail will probably go dark. Six forms cover nearly every foreign-financed project.

The mechanism, on one bridge

The same bridge, paid for three different ways. The asset is identical; the money route changes what the public owes back and which record proves the deal. The red mark is where each trail usually goes dark.

The six forms

Every form answers the same three questions: where does the money come from, what does the public owe back, and which record proves the deal. Joint ventures sit inside the equity form; power-purchase arrangements sit inside the concession form.

Form 1

Sovereign loan

What it isA foreign bank, often state-owned, lends to the government or to a company the government guarantees.
The public owesRepayment for decades, from public revenue, whatever the project earns.
Record to ask for firstThe loan agreement: rate, term, security, conditions.
Trail usually thins atThe conditions. The headline amount is announced; the terms behind it often are not.
Form 2

Concessional loan

What it isA development loan at a low headline rate, from a government agency or development bank, with conditions attached.
The public owesRepayment, plus whatever the conditions commit the country to.
Record to ask for firstThe conditions and any tied-procurement clauses.
Trail usually thins atWhat was promised in return. Cheap money is rarely only money.
Form 3

Equity stake, including joint ventures

What it isA foreign investor owns a share of the project company, alone or in a joint venture with a local or public partner.
The public owesA share of control and revenue for as long as the stake is held, sometimes with a promise to buy the output for years.
Record to ask for firstThe ownership split and the people behind the holding companies.
Trail usually thins atWho really gains. Ownership often runs through companies that disclose nothing.
Form 4

Concession (PPP), including power-purchase deals

What it isA private partner finances, builds or operates the asset under a long-term contract and carries real risk. The usual name is a public-private partnership.
The public owesPayments, guaranteed demand, or guaranteed prices, written into a contract that outlives governments.
Record to ask for firstThe contract: tariffs, guarantees, renegotiation history.
Trail usually thins atThe fiscal commitments. Risk moved off the budget has a way of coming back.
Form 5

Build-and-finance package

What it isThe foreign builder also brings the loan: construction and financing bundled into one deal, often called EPC plus finance.
The public owesRepayment of a price no one else ever quoted.
Record to ask for firstThe price breakdown and any competing bid or benchmark.
Trail usually thins atThe mark-up. Bundling buries the price comparison that tendering would have forced.
Form 6

Direct public capital

What it isA government funds and owns the asset itself, sometimes two governments each building their own side of a shared project.
The public owesNothing to a financier. The cost sits in the budget, where it can be seen.
Record to ask for firstThe budget line and the procurement record.
Trail usually thins atImplementation. With no financier to watch, delivery reporting is often the weakest record.

Why the form matters here

Four working rules connect the financing form to the assessment.

  1. The form never decides whether a project is eligible. That is settled by the three-question eligibility test; a genuine loan, stake, guarantee or public appropriation all qualify.
  2. The form decides which questions apply. A concession is scored against the standard and the PPP questions; a publicly owned project against the standard questions alone.
  3. The form tells you which record to demand first. When disclosure is missing, ask for the record the form was always most likely to hide, not the document that is easiest to name.
  4. When comparing two projects, compare like with like, or make the difference the question. A loan-financed project and an equity-financed one are not directly comparable; a good comparison asks how transparency behaves under each form.
  5. To pick the form for a real project interactively, use the workbench: choose the financing and the records, and it selects the questions the form calls for.
