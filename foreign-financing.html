Foreign financing · the shared definition

What counts as foreign financing?

A project is foreign-financed when a foreign source has made a material, signed and identifiable financial commitment to the asset. If that holds, the project enters the foreign-financing universe; the answer decides entry, and the record decides what to ask next.

It counts
  • A loan
  • An equity or ownership stake
  • A grant that funds the asset itself
  • A signed guarantee, from the moment it is signed, before any money moves
  • Another signed financial instrument
It does not count
  • A construction contract: being paid to build is revenue, not financing
  • Equipment supply
  • Consultancy or design work
  • A feasibility-study or preparatory grant: money around the project, not in the asset
  • An announcement or unsigned intention
Ask three questions
  1. Is the source foreign to the country where the asset is built?
  2. Is its money at risk or owed for the asset itself?
  3. Is the commitment signed and identifiable?

If all three are yes, the project enters the foreign-financing universe.

What "material", "identifiable", "signed" and "foreign" mean

MaterialIt funds the asset itself. Removing it would stall, shrink or materially alter the project.
IdentifiableThe financier can be named from a source and traced through holding companies to its origin.
SignedAn agreement or guarantee exists, not merely an announcement. It counts whether or not funds have yet moved.
ForeignDetermined by the origin of the money, not the builder's nationality, the currency or the equipment supplier.
Full assessment definition

A project is foreign-financed when it receives material financial support from at least one identifiable foreign source, through a loan, an equity or ownership stake, a grant that funds the asset, a guarantee, or another financial instrument, and the support is signed and documented.

  • Material: as a working reading, support whose withdrawal would not stall or shrink the asset is not material; any numeric threshold is for the methodology guide to set.
  • Identifiable: a stake held through a third-country vehicle counts by its ultimate owner, not by the vehicle's registration.
  • State-owned and private both count: a state bank, a development agency, a company or a fund; what matters is that it is foreign and identifiable.
  • Two governments building a shared project each fund their own side; each side is domestic to its own country and foreign to the other's records.

Five mistakes that misclassify projects

  1. A foreign builder is not necessarily a foreign financier. A contractor paid to build risks nothing after payment.
  2. The funder's origin matters more than the currency. A domestic loan paid in dollars is still domestic; a foreign loan in local currency is still foreign.
  3. A signed guarantee counts before money moves. Money at risk is support.
  4. A preparatory grant does not count unless it materially funds the asset. A grant that does fund the asset counts, recorded by name.
  5. Opaque ownership is a flag, not permission to exclude. Where a named financier's trail cannot be traced further, keep the project and flag it; opacity is itself a finding. Only a project with no identifiable foreign source at all stays out.

How the answer is used

This is question two of the eligibility test: a project with no qualifying foreign financing does not enter the dataset. Once a project qualifies, record its financing form, and where several foreign sources qualify, record them all; the form decides which disclosure questions apply and which record to ask for first.

Further procedural rules
  1. Record the country's own legal answer alongside the shared one. Where the two disagree, the gap is itself a finding: an obligation the country's records were never built to see.
  2. Cross-border projects: the unit is the project as it appears in the assessing country's list, scored against that country's records. A shared project may sit in both countries' lists, each side scored on its own records, and comparing the two is part of the point.
  3. The workbench, built in an earlier phase, practises the neighbouring step: given the financing, which records to demand. It does not test this definition; eligibility is settled here first.

Why Infra Watch uses one shared test

Every country's laws test foreign involvement differently, so the same project can be foreign-financed in one country's records and invisible in another's. Take one railway: built with a loan from a foreign state bank, no foreign owner, repaid as public debt. Under the shared test above it qualifies. Run it through three common legal tests instead and you get three different answers; none of the tests is wrong, they were written to protect different things. The programme does not replace any country's legal test; it adds one question every partner asks the same way, so twenty projects in one country can be compared with twenty in another.
