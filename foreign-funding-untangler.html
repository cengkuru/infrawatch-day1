One sovereign loan. One railway. The exact same financing instrument, with the exact same facts on paper. It sounds like a question with one answer.

Is this railway foreign-funded?

Hold these four facts in your head. They never change. Watch five countries give five different answers anyway.

the project, in full

project  a national railway
financing  a sovereign loan (the state borrows, the state repays)
foreign ownership stake  none
repayment  public debt

Public debt means the public pays. Every citizen helps repay this loan through the national budget, whether or not the railway ever reaches their town. So whether it gets counted as foreign-funded is not a filing detail. It decides whether anyone can hold someone to account for it.

one project, five differently-shaped filters

the machine

One project. Five rulebooks.

On paper, the railway and its loan are identical everywhere. Nothing about the project changes as it crosses a border. What changes is the shape of the filter each country holds up to decide if it counts as foreign-funded. Drop the same token through all five and watch where it comes to rest.

RO

She ran the exercise to build one common definition of foreign-funded infrastructure, so the country findings could be synthesised and compared at all. Ria Orca, BRI Monitoring Coordinator, CIPE Manila · the funding-definition session, Day 1

Kenya · the test that catches it

The public-debt register catches it.

Caught · counts as foreign-funded

Kenya's filter is wide: is this on the public-debt register? A sovereign loan is, so the token drops in and lands. The railway is logged as foreign-funded.

Source: Ria's funding-definition exercise, Nairobi Day 1. Confidence: field note, verified against the debt-register criterion.

Ghana · the test that misses it

The ownership test lets it slip.

Slips through · not counted the same way

Ghana asks something else entirely: does a foreign entity hold an ownership stake? A sovereign loan carries no such stake, so the exact same railway slips straight through and never appears as foreign-funded. The project did not change. The question did.

Source: Ria's funding-definition exercise, Nairobi Day 1. Confidence: field note, verified against the ownership-stake criterion.

Nigeria · the test the loan walks around

It never went through the Procurement Act.

Slips through · bypassed the disclosed channel

Nigeria's line runs through its Public Procurement Act: a project counts, and is disclosed, when it goes through competitive public procurement. But a direct-award foreign sovereign loan bypasses the Act entirely, the way Nigeria's Chinese-financed projects routinely do. So the same railway slips the disclosed channel and never lands in the procurement record.

DP

Nigeria's partners, self-described "glorified Excel" treasure-hunters, flagged that the Public Procurement Act of 2007 exists but Chinese-funded projects routinely bypass it, and that Auditor-General reports run about four years late, so the verdict lands long after the money is spent. Dataphyte, Nigeria partner · the funding-definition session, Day 1

Source: Ria's regulatory review (procurement domain) + Day 2 Nigeria case. Confidence: field note, verified against the procurement-bypass criterion.

Ethiopia · the test that catches it, and its blind spot

Foreign source, so it is caught. But watch the hole.

Caught · the source is foreign

Ethiopia's test is about the source of the money: foreign or domestic. A foreign sovereign loan is plainly foreign, so the railway is caught here. The catch is the blind spot the test leaves: a project financed at home, like the GERD dam at roughly 95 percent domestic, dodges the foreign-funding lens entirely and reports on its own disclosure.

MC

Dodging foreign funding does not dodge the chokehold. GERD is about 95 percent domestically funded, so Ethiopia escaped the foreign chokehold and landed in a domestic-debt one, and self-funding means the government reports on its own disclosure. Michael Cengkuru, CoST · live intervention, Day 2 (well received, tied to Ria's verification point)

Source: Ria's regulatory review (ODA/source domain) + Michael's captured Day 2 intervention. Confidence: verified.

DR Congo · the test that fires too late

It only looks at execution, not at the loan.

Unclear · not tested at the financing gate

DR Congo's lens is foreign-ownership disclosure and good governance in execution, not the financing structure. A sovereign loan carries no foreign owner to disclose, and the test only fires once the project is being built. So at the moment of the loan, the same railway returns no clear answer at all: it is not caught, not cleanly missed, just untested where it matters.

JB

CDHD frames the whole problem as a lack of transparency and good governance, especially in execution, and wants foreign ownership disclosed so an informed public can pressure duty-bearers. John Bosco, CDHD, DR Congo · the funding-definition session, Day 1 (lower transcript confidence)

Source: Ria's regulatory review (ownership/execution) + CDHD's stated frame. Confidence: field note, DRC transcript confidence lower; verified against the execution-disclosure criterion.

the reveal

The project did not change. The definition did.

Same loan. Same railway. Same facts. Drop it through the five filters and it comes to rest in five different places: caught in Kenya and Ethiopia, slipped past Ghana and Nigeria, and in DR Congo nothing caught it at all. "Foreign-funded" turned out not to be a fact about the project. It was a decision, made differently by each rulebook.

the fix

One shared definition every country maps up to.

The answer is not to rewrite five countries' laws. It is one shared, OC4IDS-anchored definition of foreign-funded infrastructure that each country maps its own records up to. Keep the national tests; add one common layer above them, so the same railway finally counts the same way everywhere, and cross-country comparison can be trusted.

The risk the CIPE leads named at the close was keeping this simple and untangled, and making it stick beyond the conference room. A shared definition is how it sticks. Catherine "Cathy" Tai, Deputy Regional Director for Asia, CIPE · plenary close, Day 1 Photo: CIPE (corrosiveconstructivecapital.cipe.org)

The Day 1 finding, in one breath

Where the filter catches this railway, it is someone's problem. Where it slips, it is no one's. Same money, same risk, same citizens repaying the loan, and a different rule decides whether anyone is accountable for it.

"Foreign-funded" is not a fact about a project. It is a decision each country's law makes differently, and the decision is what sets who can be held to account. Until there is one shared definition to map up to, no cross-country comparison can be trusted.

Ria Orca's funding-definition exercise asked each partner how their regulatory framework defines foreign funding, structured as a regulatory review across four domains: public procurement, PPPs, SOEs, and ODA (CIPE methodology guide, 21 May 2026). Each country's gate above is the domain its framework leans on, paired with the verified Day 2 case that shows the same railway's verdict.

The five tests. Kenya, the public-debt register (ODA domain): a sovereign loan is logged, so it is caught. Ghana, foreign ownership stake (PPP/SOE domain): a loan carries no foreign owner, so it slips. Nigeria, the Public Procurement Act (procurement domain): the Act of 2007 exists but Chinese-financed projects routinely bypass it via direct award, so a direct-award loan slips the disclosed channel. Ethiopia, source of financing: a foreign sovereign loan is foreign, so it is caught, though the test leaves a blind spot for the roughly 95 percent domestically financed GERD. DR Congo, foreign-ownership disclosure at execution: the test fires during construction, not at the loan, so a sovereign loan returns no clear answer at the financing gate.
