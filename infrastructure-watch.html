Infrastructure Watch · the programme in brief

Can the value of a foreign-financed project be judged from public records? That is the question, asked project by project.

The honest answer is often no, and the no is the finding. Infrastructure Watch is a programme of the Center for International Private Enterprise (CIPE), carried out with country research partners across Asia and Africa, among them Verité Research and IEA-Kenya. Its transparency data points draw on the standard of CoST, the Infrastructure Transparency Initiative. Partners do not audit projects. They test what the public record shows, and turn what is missing into precise, answerable requests. Each country list holds twenty projects; selection is the current phase.

Five shared steps; the missing record is the finding

Five shared steps: the rules are the same everywhere, applied with locally documented evidence methods. Four of the steps have pages in this definition bank; guidance for the fifth, Act, arrives with the methodology guide. The amber strip beneath the steps carries the point most worth holding: a missing record is not a failure of the method, it is the finding.

Programme steps as proposed for partner consultation, August 2026.

Three goals, in rising order of ambition

Three goals, in rising order of ambition.

Goal 1

Make foreign-financed infrastructure legible

For each project: who financed it, in what form, what the public owes back, and what the published record does and does not show. Legibility is the floor everything else stands on.

Goal 2

Make transparency comparable

One shared definition of foreign financing, one eligibility test, and one set of scoring questions, applied identically everywhere, so differences in the results reflect differences in disclosure, not differences in method.

Goal 3

Turn gaps into asks

The end product is not a league table. It is a set of precise, answerable requests: this record, for this project, from this institution. A request aimed at the exact document a financing form tends to hide is specific enough to be answered, and hard to wave away.

Concretely, each project produces a disclosure profile across the forty data points, a project result averaged over the applicable points, an evidence link behind every score, flags wherever the evidence is doubtful, and precise requests for what is missing.

What the programme is not

  • Not an audit. Partners test published records; they do not inspect projects, verify engineering, or judge value for money directly.
  • Not an accusation. Scores measure records, not motives. The PRC and non-PRC labels are attribution, and a thin record is a place to ask, not a verdict of wrongdoing.
  • Not a one-off report. The method is designed to be repeated: the same tests on the same projects show whether disclosure improves after the asking starts. When a requested record appears, the affected points can be rechecked, and the change itself becomes part of the evidence.

Where to go from here

A suggested reading order for a new partner: first the eligibility test, then foreign financing, the financing forms, the 40 data points and the 0 to 6 scale; then the specialist pages as they arise: PPP, power purchase agreements, PRC and non-PRC, and the buffer list.
