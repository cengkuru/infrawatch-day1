Oversight publication · does an audit or parliamentary source earn the score?

An oversight body earns the credit for the point it was mandated to publish, and for no other

An audit institution's published audit report is the routine disclosure of the audit point. Parliament publishing the appropriation is the routine disclosure of the budget point. Both score in full. What neither can do is stand in for the delivery system's disclosure of a different point.

Never score a fact because scrutiny dragged it into the open. A contract price that became public only through an audit, investigation, court case or parliamentary inquiry shows that oversight worked, not that disclosure did.

Two checks decide it

Applied per data point, never per publisher: the same audit report can pass for one point and fail for the next.

Check 1Does this body hold the mandate for this data point?An audit institution is mandated to publish audits; Parliament is mandated to publish appropriations. Mandate is read per point, never granted to a body wholesale.
Check 2Did the information appear as routine practice, or did scrutiny surface it?Routine publication scores. Anything extracted by audit, investigation, adjudication or inquiry is recorded as oversight-related availability instead.

What counts, and what does not

Scores in full
  • An audit institution's published audit report, scored against the audit point.
  • Parliament's published appropriation, scored against the budget point.
  • A PPP unit or other mandated publisher issuing the record on the owner's behalf.
Recorded, not scored
  • A contract price visible only inside an audit report. Wrong mandate.
  • Project costs released through a court judgment or inquiry. Scrutiny, not disclosure.
  • Figures a committee obtained by summons. Extracted, not published.

Worked example: the audit that carries two facts

  1. Starting facts. An audit institution publishes an audit of a road project, containing the audit findings and a signed contract price that appears nowhere else.
  2. Rule applied. Check 1 passes for the audit point and fails for the price: the audit body holds no mandate to publish contract prices.
  3. Result. The audit point scores on the audit report. The contract price scores zero against the responsible authority's record.
  4. Recorded. The price is kept in the evidence base with its source and date, labelled oversight-related availability, and the divergence is flagged as a finding.

What happens next

Mandate holds: score the point in full, and record which body published it. Mandate does not hold: retain the evidence, label it oversight-related availability, and score that point against the responsible authority's own record. Mandate is unclear: flag it for a ruling rather than deciding it in the field.
Why the gap is retained rather than discarded

A point available only through oversight describes a specific kind of government: scrutiny functions, proactive disclosure does not. Discarding the evidence would erase that finding and leave the record indistinguishable from a country where the information does not exist at all. The retention rule follows the same logic as funder evidence on qualifying government publication: a ceiling on credit is not a reason to leave evidence out.
