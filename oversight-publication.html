Oversight publication · which point does this source earn credit for?

Credit follows the mandate, one data point at a time

An audit institution publishing its audit, or Parliament publishing the appropriation, is a qualifying government publication for that point: it enters the official band and is then scored on the normal 0 to 6 criteria. What neither can do is carry credit to a different point that happens to appear in the same document.

Never let a fact earn disclosure credit because scrutiny dragged it into the open. A price that reached the public only through an audit, court case or inquiry shows that oversight worked, not that the responsible authority published.

One audit report, two very different facts

  1. What you have. An audit institution publishes an audit of a road project, containing the audit findings and a signed contract price that appears nowhere else.
  2. The audit findings. Publishing audits is this body's mandate, so the report qualifies for that point. Score it on the normal 0 to 6 criteria.
  3. The contract price. The same body has no mandate to publish prices, so the report earns that point no credit. Assess it against the responsible authority's own record on the usual applicability, due-date and search rules.
  4. Either way, the price is kept. Recorded with its source and date, labelled oversight-related availability.

The rule, and the three situations it produces

Read the mandate per data point, never per body: one document can qualify for one point and not the next.

Qualifies for that point
  • An audit institution's published audit, for the audit point.
  • Parliament's published appropriation, for the budget point.
No credit from that source
  • A contract price visible only inside an audit report.
  • Project costs released through a court judgment, inquiry or summons.
Mandate holds: treat it as a qualifying government publication and apply the 0 to 6 criteria. Mandate does not hold: retain and label the information, and score that point against the responsible authority's record on the normal rules. Mandate is unclear: refer it for a ruling rather than deciding it in the field.

The gap is the finding

A fact the public can reach only because scrutiny forced it out describes a particular government: oversight works, routine disclosure does not. Discard it and that country reads exactly like one where the information never existed. Hence the label, on the logic that keeps funder evidence under qualifying government publication: a limit on credit is never a reason to lose evidence.

Assessor notes: naming the points, and the ledger record

Enter the audit and budget points by their scoring-workbook identifiers rather than the shorthand used above; the shorthand names the mandate, not the field.
