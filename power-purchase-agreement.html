Power purchase agreements · a one-page guide

A promise to buy power is a public commitment, spread over decades

In many power projects the government never borrows and never owns anything. It signs a contract instead: the public utility promises to buy the electricity a privately built plant produces, at an agreed price, typically for twenty years or more. That promise is what this page explains, because the promise is where the public commitment lives, paid through tariffs, subsidy or the backing the state stands behind.

The mechanism

Follow the power one way and the money the other. The plant is private; the obligation is public. The red mark is the contract, because the contract, not the plant, is where the deal lives.

The three things to hold on to

What the agreement is, why it counts as public, and what to ask for when the disclosure is thin.

What it is

A contract that makes a private plant financeable

The dealA public utility signs a long-term contract, usually twenty years or more, to buy the electricity a privately built plant produces at an agreed price.
Why it existsBanks rarely lend hundreds of millions against a plant that might find no buyer. The promise of a buyer, in writing, is what unlocks the financing. The contract builds the plant.
The plain namePower purchase agreement, or PPA. Projects built this way are often called independent power producers.
Why it counts here

The obligation is public even when nothing else is

What the public owesPayment at the agreed price for the life of the contract. If demand falls, if cheaper power arrives, if the currency moves, the promise usually holds until it is renegotiated, and renegotiations are themselves records to ask for.
In the eligibility testThe plant delivers a public service, and the contract is a real public-sector role: the long-term private partner path. These projects are scored against the standard and the PPP questions. The buyer counts as public when it is a government body or state-linked company, the same test as eligibility question 3; a regulated but privately owned utility is not a public buyer, and such a project qualifies only through binding government backing.
The distinction to keepA PPA makes the obligation public. It does not make the plant public. Say which one you mean; the records differ.
What to ask for

The contract, before anything else

Record to ask for firstThe PPA terms: the price per unit, the contract length, and any clause that pays the plant whether the power is used or not (the take-or-pay clause).
ThenThe currency of payment, who pays when demand falls, any government guarantee behind the utility, and the renegotiation history.
Trail usually thins atThe tariff and the take-or-pay terms. The plant's opening is public; the price the public locked in for decades often is not.

The boundary: which PPA projects belong in the dataset

The public promise is the test. Two cases sit at the edge.

  1. No public buyer, no public role: out of scope. A plant that sells its power strictly to a private buyer, with no public utility contract and no government backing, delivers no public service and carries no public obligation. It fails the eligibility test and stays out. The positive side of the same rule: a binding government guarantee or equivalent public backing qualifies a project even without a public purchase contract, because the public exposure is real either way.
  2. Public role thinner than a purchase promise: record the open question, do not force the call. Where the state's involvement is real but softer than a purchase obligation, or the contract cannot yet be located to confirm which it is, the project is not treated as confirmed either way: the eligibility answer is recorded as unverified until the contract is found. If a listed project later fails this scrutiny, the buffer list supplies its documented substitute.

Where this sits in the wider guidance

  1. In the eligibility test, a PPA-backed plant passes as public infrastructure delivering a public service; the contract is the public-sector role.
  2. In the financing forms, these projects sit inside the concession form: a private partner finances, builds and operates, and carries real risk, under a contract that outlives governments. Within that form, the PPA is the obligation mechanism, never the financing itself.
  3. The foreign financing behind the plant, the loan or the equity stake, is tested separately under the shared definition. A PPA explains the public role; it says nothing about where the money came from.
