The project result · how one number is built

An average over the points that apply, so an excluded point never drags the score

Each project's finding is the full profile of its scores: forty answers, each on its own merits. The project result is the summary statistic over that profile: add the scores for every applicable point, divide by the number of points that apply. Equal weight, and nothing that should not count is counted.

Thirty-three applicable points, sum 99, result 3.0

A project with the full 40 core points, of which seven should not be scored at its current stage.

Core points40
Verified not yet due: excluded, with expected stage recorded−5
Verified non-applicable: excluded, with reason and evidence−2
Applicable points, the denominator33
Sum of the 33 scores, including every due-but-unpublished zero99
Project result99 ÷ 33 = 3.0

Genuine zeros count; excluded points never do

  1. The denominator is applicable points only. Verified not-yet-due and non-applicable points leave the calculation entirely; their evidence states are recorded, and they never appear as zeros.
  2. Genuine zeros count. A due-but-unpublished point scores 0 and stays in the average: that is the disclosure failure the result exists to reflect.
  3. Equal weight per point. No point matters more than another; the working interpretation is equal weighting unless CIPE specifies otherwise. A phase with more applicable points therefore carries more of the average.
  4. Phased projects score per awarded contract. One project, three funders, three contract awards: three scores, each on its own merits, because a single blended score hides which phase's disclosure failed. Whole-project documents, the feasibility study or the environmental assessment, count toward every phase.
  5. The profile stays the finding. The result summarises; it does not replace the forty answers. Two projects can share a 3.0 with entirely different failures underneath, and the analysis reads the points, not just the mean.
The common trap: reading the result as a project grade. A 5.1 does not mean a good project, and a 1.4 does not mean a bad one; the number summarises how findable the official record is, point by point, and nothing else. The second trap is quieter: scoring a not-yet-due point as zero "to be safe". That silently moves the point into the denominator and punishes a government for a record that cannot exist yet.
Open for Phase 2: how scores from multiple contracts combine into one project result. The per-awarded-contract rule above and this roll-up rule are the same decision seen from two directions and will be settled together, with partner-specific scenarios. Until then, contract-level results are reported side by side, not blended.
