Public-private partnership · the unified definition

A partnership is defined by risk and time, not by its label

In a PPP, a private company helps deliver a public service over many years and shares the risk. Because these deals create long-lived obligations for governments that rarely appear in a budget line, the assessment needs a clear way to identify them. Every country's law defines a PPP differently, and some do not define it at all, so the programme applies one definition everywhere, whatever the contract calls itself.

Three ideas define a partnership

Before any checklist, this is what makes an arrangement a PPP.

A long-term contractA public body and a private partner bind themselves for years, usually decades, not for a single delivery.
The partner runs something and carries riskThe private side manages an important part of the asset or service, and can lose money if usage falls or performance slips.
Payment runs over timeThe partner earns from use, availability or performance across the contract's life, not from a lump sum at handover.

For consistent assessment across countries, the programme records these three ideas through six evidence checks.

Is this a PPP for this assessment?

Answer the six checks below, in any order. A project is a PPP only when every answer is yes. Do not rely on the contract title.

Check 1Is there a public authority on one side and a private partner on the other?A government body as the public party and a commercial partner on the other side; two bodies of the same government contracting each other is not a PPP.
Check 2Is the relationship long-term and contractual?The obligation outlives construction by years, typically a decade or more; a construction contract that ends at handover does not qualify.
Check 3Does the private partner contribute to financing, development, operation or maintenance?The partner puts something of its own into the asset's life, beyond being paid for a delivered service.
Check 4Does the private partner carry significant management responsibility?The partner makes operating decisions rather than performing specified tasks; a passive lender does not qualify.
Check 5Is the private partner exposed to substantial project risk?The partner can lose money when usage falls or performance slips; where the state absorbs every downside, it is procurement, not partnership.
Check 6Is payment tied to long-term performance, availability or use?The partner earns over time from tolls, tariffs, availability payments or output, not from a lump sum at delivery.
Guidance for unusual cases
  • State-owned enterprises as the commercial partner. A foreign state-owned enterprise acting as the commercial partner sits on the private side of Check 1; its state linkage is recorded through the classification, never by failing the check. Where state linkage is debatable, the deciding question is responsibility: which entity answers to the public if the project fails.
  • Special-purpose vehicles. Even where an SPV finances, builds, operates or temporarily holds title, the public entity that approved the project and answers for the service remains the project owner. The contracting authority, the legal title holder, the SPV and the beneficial owners are recorded as separate fields.
  • Projects with several contracts. The checks run per arrangement: a project holding several contracts is classified by the arrangement that creates the long-term obligation, consistent with the per-awarded-contract scoring ruling.
  • The exact long-term floor. A decade or more is the working reading for Check 2; the precise floor is the methodology guide's to set.

What happens next?

All six answers are yesIt is a PPP. Score the standard project information and the PPP questions (rationale and value-for-money evidence, risk allocation, guarantees, tariffs, performance, renegotiations, termination). The assessment tests whether that evidence was published, never whether the project was good value.
Any answer is noIt is not a PPP, whatever the title says. Score the standard project information that applies, and record why the PPP test was not met; the reason is often itself informative.
A document is missingMark that check unverified, not failed. Record the gap and continue with the PPP questions: the contract that would answer the check is itself a PPP disclosure record, so its absence is the first finding.
The evidence exists but does not settle the answerFlag it for a CIPE decision. Never squeeze it into the definition or stretch the definition around it. This is distinct from unverified: unverified means the evidence is missing; referred means the evidence is there and genuinely ambiguous.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • The contract title does not decide PPP status. Concessions, build-operate-transfer structures and independent power producers under a power purchase agreement can all be PPPs when the six checks hold, and a contract labelled "PPP" may fail them. The checks are the evidence; the title never is.
  • PPP status and foreign financing are separate questions. The six checks say nothing about where the money comes from; that is settled by the shared foreign-financing definition. A PPP can be entirely domestic, and a foreign-financed project need not be a PPP.
  • Shared risk can still be a PPP. Risk is rarely all on one side. The referral route above exists for the case where most meaningful management and performance risk returns to government; it is not a licence to refer every close call.
Assessor notes: architecture and rationale
  • Six checks, three dimensions, one instrument. The mapping report groups the six screening questions into three evidence dimensions: long-term engagement, private management and risk, and performance-linked remuneration. That grouping summarises the same instrument; a project cannot pass one and fail the other.
  • Why the extra PPP questions exist. The standard data points cannot see a PPP's hidden obligations: the guarantees, contingent liabilities and long payment promises that never appear in a budget line. The PPP questions exist to reach them; how they bind to the forty data points is being settled in the methodology note.
  • Referral authority. The ambiguous-case rule comes from the mapping report (Section 4.5.1); CIPE holds final methodology authority.
