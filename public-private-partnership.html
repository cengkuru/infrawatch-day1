Public-private partnership · the unified definition

A partnership is defined by risk and time, not by its label

Every country's law defines a public-private partnership differently, and some do not define it at all. So the programme applies one definition everywhere, the same move as with foreign financing: six tests, and an arrangement is a PPP only when all six hold, whatever the contract calls itself.

Six gates, three outcomes

The amber outcome does the most work: a test that cannot be answered from available records is unverified, never failed.

The six tests

These reproduce the screening questions in the partner assessment workbook. Run them on the arrangement as it actually operates, not on its title.

Test 1Is there a public authority on one side and a private partner on the other?A government body or state-linked company as the public party, and a commercial partner on the other side. Where state linkage is debatable, the deciding question is responsibility: which entity answers to the public if the project fails. A foreign state-owned enterprise acting as the commercial partner sits on the private side of this test, its state linkage recorded through the classification, never by failing this test. Two public bodies of the same government contracting each other is government to government, not a PPP.
Test 2Is the relationship long-term and contractual, rather than short-term or ad hoc?Concessions and purchase agreements run for decades. As a working reading, long-term means the obligation outlives construction by years, typically a decade or more; the exact floor is the methodology guide's to set. A construction contract that ends at handover fails this test.
Test 3Does the private partner contribute to financing, development, operation or maintenance?The partner puts something of its own into the asset's life, beyond being paid for a delivered service.
Test 4Does the private partner carry significant management responsibility?It runs something: the asset, the service, the revenue collection. The anchor: the partner makes operating decisions, not merely performs specified tasks, which is what separates a concessionaire from an outsourced maintenance crew. A passive lender fails this test.
Test 5Is the private partner exposed to substantial project risk?Demand, performance or cost risk that actually lands on the partner. The anchor: if the partner can lose money when usage falls or performance slips, the test holds. Where the state absorbs every downside, there is no partnership, only procurement with extra steps.
Test 6Is payment tied to long-term performance, availability or use?The partner earns over time from tolls, tariffs, availability payments or output, not from a lump sum at delivery.
All six holdIt is a PPP for assessment purposes: score the standard 40 data points plus the PPP questions (published rationale and value-for-money evidence, risk allocation, ownership and beneficial-ownership structures, guarantees and contingent liabilities, tariffs and payment mechanisms, performance and penalties, renegotiations, termination and handover). The assessment tests whether that evidence was published, never whether the project was good value. The full question set lives in the assessment workbook and the methodology note in preparation.
Any test failsIt is not a PPP for assessment purposes, whatever its title says; a contract's title is never evidence, the tests are. Score the standard data points that apply. Record which test failed; the reason is often itself informative.
A test cannot be answeredRecord it as unverified, never as failed, flag the project, and run the PPP questions anyway: the contract that would answer the test is itself a PPP disclosure record, so its absence is the first finding, not a reason to exempt the project from scrutiny.

The rules that keep it comparable

  • The public contracting authority stays the project owner. Even where a special-purpose vehicle finances, builds, operates or temporarily holds title, the public entity that approved the project and answers for the service remains the owner. Otherwise "owner" would mean different things across projects and comparability dies. For assessment, the contracting authority, the legal title holder, the SPV and the beneficial owners are recorded as separate fields; the owner rule fixes who counts as owner, it never erases the others. The tests run per arrangement: a project holding several contracts is classified by the arrangement that creates the long-term obligation, consistent with the per-awarded-contract scoring ruling.
  • The family is bigger than the name. Concessions, build-operate-transfer structures and independent power producers under a power purchase agreement all sit in this family when the six tests hold. So can a state-owned enterprise: an SOE that carries the management and risk sits on the private side of the tests, whoever owns it.
  • The hard case is referred, never made to fit. Shared risk is compatible with a PPP. But where the tests can be answered and the answer is that most meaningful management and performance risk returns to government, the arrangement is recorded as an analytical finding and referred to CIPE for a decision, rather than squeezed into the definition or the definition stretched around it. This is the mapping report's rule for the ambiguous case, distinct from the unverified path above: unverified means the evidence is missing; referred means the evidence is there and points between the categories.
  • Six tests, three dimensions, one instrument. The mapping report groups these six screening questions into three evidence dimensions: long-term engagement, private management and risk, and performance-linked remuneration. That grouping is a summary of the same instrument, not a second test, so a project cannot pass one and fail the other.
  • PPP status and foreign financing are separate questions. The six tests say nothing about where the money comes from; that is settled by the shared foreign-financing definition. A PPP can be entirely domestic, and a foreign-financed project need not be a PPP.
  • Why the extra questions exist: the standard data points cannot see a PPP's hidden obligations, the guarantees, contingent liabilities and long payment promises that never appear in a budget line. The PPP questions exist to reach them; how they bind to the forty is being settled in the methodology note.
