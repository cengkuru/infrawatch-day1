Qualifying government publication · who gets the credit

A document on the internet is not a document the government chose to publish

Ask who wrote the document, not where you found it. Scores 3 to 6 on the scale reward official disclosure, so every source must answer one question first: does this count as the government publishing? Two rules exist side by side, one operative today and one proposed, and partners need to know both, because funder-portal documents are everywhere.

Authorship decides today; the channel test awaits CIPE

The current rule decides today's scores. The proposed refinement exists because authorship alone may over-credit a document hosted only by a funder.

Current rule · settled and operative

Government authorship decides

A government-authored publication may receive government credit, wherever it is hosted: authorship decides, hosting never does. Funder-only or other third-party publication remains evidence, is labelled separately, and is capped at 2 on the 0 to 6 scale.

The funder cap was confirmed in writing on 16 July 2026 and reconfirmed on 21 July 2026.

CoST proposal · awaiting CIPE confirmation

Intentional publication through an official channel

The refinement would require intentional publication through an official or legally designated government channel, within the publishing body's mandate. A government-authored file that only a funder hosts would no longer earn full official credit on its own.

Not operative unless CIPE confirms it.

The recorded rulings

The boundary cases, under the rule in force today.

The caseUnder the current rule
A government-authored document hosted on an external site, a funder page or a file-sharing linkOfficial; can reach 6. This is exactly the case the proposed refinement targets, so record the hosting.
A funder's own page quoting government figuresFunder-only evidence. Labelled separately, caps at 2.
A loan agreement ratified by parliament and published through Hansard or the gazetteQualifying government publication. Ghana's ratification channel is the report's named example.

A machine-readable dataset versus a scanned PDFMachine readability distinguishes the upper score levels; it never decides who gets attribution.

Four habits keep attribution defensible

  1. Classify the source before scoring the fact. The publication-control question comes first; the rung on the scale comes second.
  2. Record publisher, scope and date for every figure, so a change of rule later can be applied without redoing the search.
  3. Funder evidence is kept, never discarded. The cap at 2 is a ceiling on credit, not a reason to leave the evidence out of the record.
  4. Uncertain authorship is a flag, not a guess. Where who authored or controls a publication cannot be established, record what is known and flag it; flags never change scores.
The common trap: reading "it is public" as "the government published it". A complete, accurate project page on a development bank's website proves the fact is knowable; it says nothing about whether the government made it findable, and that second question is the one the whole assessment measures.
