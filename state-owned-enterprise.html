State-owned enterprises · the agreed classification rule

An SOE is classified by what it does in the contract, not by who owns it

State-owned enterprises appear everywhere in the cohort: as concession holders, as equity investors, as builders, as implementing agencies. The ownership label answers none of the questions that matter. The agreed rule classifies each SOE by its contractual role, management responsibility and risk, with the supporting evidence recorded.

One enterprise, four possible roles

The same kind of entity lands on different sides of the same assessment, and the contract decides each time. The examples are the report's own diagnostic classifications.

Role in the contractClassificationWorked example
The private partnerA foreign SOE that develops, finances and operates under a concession carries private-partner risk, so the arrangement is a PPP, whoever owns the enterprise.Kenya's Nairobi Expressway: a concession developed, financed and operated by a foreign state-owned enterprise. Confirmed a PPP.
The equity investorA foreign SOE holding an equity stake in a concessionaire is a financing party, recorded in the attribution record with its ultimate control.The Philippines' national grid: a foreign SOE acting under a concession through an equity stake. Confirmed a PPP; the project owner stays the granting public authority.
The lender's ownerA state export-import bank lending to a government is sovereign lending: public debt financing, not a PPP, however state-owned the bank is.Kenya's Standard Gauge Railway: sovereign loans from a state bank to the government. Confirmed not a PPP.
The public sideA domestic SOE can be the implementing body or even the contracting side; the responsible public authority is still identified by answerability, and the SOE's role is recorded.Nepal's airport and hydropower projects: sovereign loans implemented by public bodies or state-owned enterprises. Not PPPs.

The test, in order

  1. Ignore the ownership label. "State-owned" tells you who holds the shares, not what the contract does. Set it aside.
  2. Read the contractual role. Does the enterprise carry management responsibility and a meaningful share of performance or project risk? Then it functions as a private partner, and the PPP tests apply.
  3. Or is it paying, or being paid? An SOE lending is a financier; an SOE building for a fee is a contractor, and a builder's flag never decides classification.
  4. Record the evidence. The responsibility matrix, the risk allocation, the payment mechanism: the classification carries its supporting evidence, so two assessors reach the same answer.

Why the rule exists

  1. Ownership-based classification breaks the comparison. Chinese infrastructure finance has moved toward concessions, equity stakes and joint ventures, often through SOEs. Classify those by ownership and they all collapse into "government", erasing the very arrangements the programme exists to examine.
  2. The same label points both ways. An SOE can sit on the public side in one project and carry private-partner risk in the next; only the role distinguishes them.
  3. Role-based classification is reproducible. Two analysts reading the same contract evidence reach the same answer; two analysts guessing from an ownership label do not.
The common trap: "state-owned, so it is the government side". Kenya's expressway concessionaire and the Philippines' grid investor are both state-owned, and both sit on the private side of their contracts, carrying the management and risk that make the arrangements PPPs. The mirror trap also fails: a foreign SOE's involvement does not make a project sovereign lending; the instrument does.
