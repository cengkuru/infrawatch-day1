Sufficient search · the floor under every zero

An unsearched point is never a zero

A zero says the government failed to publish. That claim is only as strong as the search behind it, so the search itself is part of the evidence: completed across the applicable government channels, and recorded. An evidenced absence is a scoring result; an unsearched point is not.

The settled definition: a completed, recorded search

One of the five controlled terms the mapping report's Annex B fixes for assessors.

A sufficient search is a completed search of the government portals and official published records applicable to the data point, with the searches themselves recorded. An unsearched point is not a zero.

Infra Watch mapping report, Annex B, operational terms

What "applicable channels" means

The report's source inventory (Annex D) lists each partner country's channels. A sufficient search covers the ones this data point could plausibly live in, not every site in the country.

Channels that decide the score

  • The PPP unit or PPP disclosure portal, where one exists.
  • The procurement portal for tender and award points.
  • Debt, budget and aid databases for financing points.
  • The responsible ministry's own pages, gazettes and parliamentary records: Ghana's loan ratifications, for example, publish through Hansard and the gazette.

Channels that are evidence, never the government record

  • Funder project pages and lender-side databases.
  • Third-party trackers and research databases.
  • Press coverage quoting official figures.
  • These belong in the search and in the record, but what they find is not government publication and caps at 2 on the scale.

What the search record holds

The record is what lets a reviewer, or a challenged government, reconstruct the search. As working practice, record for each searched point:

  1. Which channels were searched, by name and address, drawn from the country's source inventory.
  2. When each search was run: addresses and holdings change, so the date is part of the claim.
  3. What was searched for: the project identifiers and terms used, including host-language terms.
  4. What was found or not found, with the source, publisher, scope and date of anything found.

One search, worked through

A budget point for a Cambodian expressway concession, a country the report notes has no consolidated portal, shows what sufficiency looks like where portal infrastructure is thin.

  1. Start from the inventory. Cambodia's listed channels: the MEF General Department of PPP site, and the MEF public debt statistical bulletins. No consolidated tender portal is identified.
  2. Search each channel for the project by name and by concession, in English and Khmer, and record each search with its date.
  3. Extend to the responsible authority. The contracting institution's own pages and any gazette or official announcement channel it uses.
  4. Record the funder's figures separately. The concession is documented mainly by its financiers and the press; that evidence is kept, labelled, and capped at 2. It does not end the search for the government record.
  5. Close the search. Nothing qualifying found: the point is due, the search is complete and recorded, and only now does the field take a zero, recorded as due but unpublished with the search record as its evidence.
The common trap: stopping when something is found. Finding the budget on the funder's project page answers a different question; the assessment measures what the government made findable, so the search is complete only when the government's own channels have been covered and recorded. The second trap is the quick look: one portal, one language, no record. Neither supports a zero, and a zero without a search record is an insufficiently searched point, not a finding.
