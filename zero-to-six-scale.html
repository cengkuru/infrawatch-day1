The 0 to 6 scale · how every fact is scored

The score measures the public record, never the project

For each fact about a project, the scale asks one question: how far can an ordinary person travel toward it? Seven values, numbered 0 to 6, from nothing found to the official document itself. A high score means the government made the fact easy to reach. It says nothing about whether the project is good.

The ladder

Each rung, quoted from the Infra Watch Transparency Assessment score sheet that partners score in, with the plain reading underneath. The ladder is sequential: a fact climbs until it fails, and stays at the last rung it passed.

Rungs 0 to 2 · not yet official disclosure
0
Nothing foundSheet wording: "No information available." Scored only after a reasonable, completed search, not after a quick look.
1
Online, somewhereSheet wording: "The information is available online, in an open format, and without the need for request."
2
Readable, from anyoneSheet wording: "The information is available in publicly available websites and uses English or host-country language." Any publisher counts here: a newspaper, a researcher, a contractor, a funder. A government source published only in a language that is neither English nor a host-country language is an edge case flagged for a ruling.
Rungs 3 to 6 · official disclosure starts here (the sheet's own binary line)
3
The government itself published itSheet wording: "The information is available on the website/s of the host government or project owner." Any government-controlled channel counts: a ministry site, a procurement or budget portal, a debt database, an audit institution.
4
And it is clearSheet wording adds: "in a fairly easy to understand format." A citizen gets the answer without mining hundreds of pages or assembling fragments.
5
And it is consolidatedSheet wording adds: the same official source "contains at least 10 other data points", rewarding governments that gather disclosure in one place instead of scattering it.
6
And the document itself is thereSheet wording adds: "an official full document related to the data point is provided", meaning the signed contract, the feasibility study, the audit, the evaluation report.
The common trap: a funder's project page, however accurate and complete, is not government disclosure. It caps the score at 2, because the question is what the host government has made public, not what can be learned about the project somewhere.

One fact, up the ladder

A generic example: the project budget for an imagined road project, scored at each stage the record could be in.

  1. Score 2. The budget appears in the funder's project PDF, in English. Nothing on any government channel. Readable, from anyone, but not official, so it stops here.
  2. Score 3. The finance ministry's investment portal lists the project with its budget. Official disclosure begins.
  3. Score 4. The portal states the budget plainly on the project page, one figure with its approval date, no mining required.
  4. Score 5. The same portal also carries the project's location, contracts, timeline and a dozen other data points. Consolidated.
  5. Score 6. The approved budget document itself is attached. The reader holds the source, not a summary of it.
  6. And the other direction: the budget was approved and due, a completed search finds nothing anywhere. Score 0, recorded as 0-D.

Who controls the publication? The recorded rulings

The boundary cases where honest scorers disagree, settled by one principle: authorship decides, hosting never does.

The caseThe ruling
A government-authored document hosted on an external site, a funder page or a file-sharing linkOfficial. Can reach 6.
A funder's own page quoting government figuresCaps at 2.
A government-authored article printed in a newspaperOfficial.
Information available only on requestFails the no-request test.

The working rules

  1. Sequential, no skipping. A fact stops at the first rung it fails; a 6 requires everything beneath it to hold.
  2. Publisher control decides "official". Classify a source by who controls the publication, not by which website hosts it. National portals and government databases are official disclosure and can earn 3 to 6; a third-party page never becomes official merely by being correct.
  3. Zero is a verdict, not a default. Score 0 only when the fact is applicable, already due at the project's stage, and a reasonable, completed search found nothing. What counts as a reasonable search is being defined, with worked examples, in the methodology guide.
  4. Not applicable is not zero. A fact that is not yet due at this stage is recorded N/A-T; one that does not apply to this kind of project is recorded N/A-M. Both leave the calculation entirely. A genuine zero is recorded 0-D, so a blank cell never has to be guessed at.
  5. A disclosed "none" is an answer. An official statement that "no households were displaced" is a complete, scoreable disclosure like any other, never a zero.
  6. Doubts become flags, never score changes. Conflicting figures, undated pages, unstable links: these are recorded as flags beside the score, so the scale stays clean and the doubts stay visible.

What gets scored

The scale is applied to each of the 40 data points separately, so one project produces a profile of scores, not a single grade. Projects that pass the PPP definition are additionally assessed on the PPP questions.
