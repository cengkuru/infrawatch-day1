The 0 to 6 scale · how every fact is scored

The score measures the public record, never the project

For each fact about a project, the scale asks one question: has the government made it public, and how easy is it to reach? Seven values, numbered 0 to 6. A high score means the government made the fact easy to find. It says nothing about whether the project is good.

Has the government made this fact public?

The seven scores fall into three zones. Grasp the zones and you have the scale.

0
Not found

A completed search of the applicable government channels found nothing, for a fact that was due and applicable.

1–2
Available, but not official

The fact can be found online, in a readable language, but the government has not published it. The publisher might be a newspaper, a researcher, a contractor or a funder.

3–6
Official government disclosure

The government itself published the fact. Higher scores add clarity (4), consolidation with other project data (5), and the official document itself (6).

Score 3 is the turning point. Below it, the fact may be findable, but the government has not made it public. What counts as "the government published it" is decided by who controls the publication, never by which website hosts it: a government document hosted on a funder's site is still official, and a funder's page quoting government figures is not. The recorded rulings live on the qualifying government publication page.
Important: a funder's project page does not count as government disclosure. However accurate and complete the World Bank or AfDB page is, it caps the score at 2, because the question is what the host government has made public, not what can be learned about the project somewhere.

Worked example: one project budget at every score

The budget of an imagined road project, at each stage the record could be in.

  1. Score 2. The budget appears in the funder's project PDF, in English. Nothing on any government channel. Readable, from anyone, but not official, so it stops here.
  2. Score 3. The finance ministry's investment portal lists the project with its budget. Official disclosure begins.
  3. Score 4. The portal states the budget plainly on the project page, one figure with its approval date, no mining required.
  4. Score 5. The same portal also carries the project's location, contracts, timeline and a dozen other data points. Consolidated.
  5. Score 6. The approved budget document itself is attached. The reader holds the source, not a summary of it.
  6. And the other direction: the budget was approved and due, and a completed search finds nothing anywhere. Score 0.

When you need to assign the exact score

The full seven-value reference. The ladder is sequential: a fact climbs until it fails, and stays at the last score it passed.

Scores 0 to 2 · not yet official disclosure
0
Nothing foundNo information available anywhere, after a reasonable, completed search, not after a quick look.
1
Online, somewhereThe information is available online, in an open format, without needing to request it.
2
Readable, from anyoneAvailable on a publicly accessible website, in English or a host-country language. Any publisher counts here: a newspaper, a researcher, a contractor, a funder.
Scores 3 to 6 · official disclosure starts here
3
The government itself published itOn a website of the host government or project owner. Any government-controlled channel counts: a ministry site, a procurement or budget portal, a debt database, an audit institution.
4
And it is clearIn a fairly easy-to-understand format: a citizen gets the answer without mining hundreds of pages or assembling fragments.
5
And it is consolidatedThe same official source carries at least ten other data points, rewarding governments that gather disclosure in one place instead of scattering it.
6
And the document itself is thereAn official full document related to the data point is provided: the signed contract, the feasibility study, the audit, the evaluation report.
Assessment wording, verbatim
  • 0: "No information available."
  • 1: "The information is available online, in an open format, and without the need for request."
  • 2: "The information is available in publicly available websites and uses English or host-country language." A government source published only in a language that is neither English nor a host-country language is an edge case flagged for a ruling.
  • 3: "The information is available on the website/s of the host government or project owner."
  • 4 adds: "in a fairly easy to understand format."
  • 5 adds: the same official source "contains at least 10 other data points".
  • 6 adds: "an official full document related to the data point is provided".

Do not make these scoring mistakes

  1. A score of 0 requires a completed search. Score 0 only when the fact is applicable, already due at the project's stage, and a sufficient search of the applicable government channels found nothing. An unsearched point is never a zero.
  2. A funder page can inform the record but cannot earn official-disclosure scores. Classify a source by who controls the publication, not by which website hosts it. A third-party page never becomes official merely by being correct.
  3. Not applicable and not yet due are excluded, not scored zero. A fact that does not apply, or is not yet due at this stage, leaves the calculation entirely. Only a due, applicable, searched-and-absent fact is a genuine zero. The full label set lives in evidence states.
Further scoring rules
  • Sequential, no skipping. A fact stops at the first score it fails; a 6 requires everything beneath it to hold.
  • A disclosed "none" is an answer. An official statement that "no households were displaced" is a complete, scoreable disclosure, never a zero.
  • Doubts become flags, never score changes. Conflicting figures, undated pages, unstable links are recorded as flags beside the score, so the scale stays clean and the doubts stay visible.
  • Recording labels. A genuine zero is recorded 0-D; not-yet-due is N/A-T; not applicable to this kind of project is N/A-M. Details in evidence states.

From forty scores to one result

The scale is applied to each of the 40 data points separately, and the project result is the equal-weight average over the applicable points; excluded points never drag it down. Projects that meet the PPP definition are additionally assessed on the PPP questions.
